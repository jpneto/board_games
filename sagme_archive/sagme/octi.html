Octi

Date: 2027-11-26 16:46 | Tags: checkers, new games, stack pieces

Octi is a 1999 game by Don Green, published at FoxMind (among others).

The game is played on a 9x9 board, with three home bases on each side (the black squares labeled Octi):

Each player has seven pieces (the pods) and 25 prongs that can be added to the pods

Initially, three pods per player are placed on the respective home bases.

Rules for Fast Octi variant:

  • On his turn, the player either:
    • add a prong into a friendly pod
    • drop a pod in reserve on an empty home base
    • move a pod one step in the direction of one of its prongs, to a square not occupied by an enemy pod
      • a pod can move on top of a friendly pod, making a stack
    • use a pod to jump over (in the direction of one of its prongs) an adjacent pod of either color, landing on the immediate next cell that must be empty
      • jumps can be multiple, but the piece cannot jump into the same square twice
      • any jumped pod might be captured (including friendly pieces), and its prongs belong now to the attacker
    • move/jump all pieces of a stack (either pod accordingly to its prongs)
      • these moves are considered simultaneous (pods from the same stack cannot jump each other, for example)
  • Wins the player that occupies an enemy home base

Octi is a game with enormous branching factor, once pods and prongs start to populate the board. The game idea is quite interesting, and it is a worthwhile addition, even if quite exotic, to the Checkers family.

Stephen also wrote a review of the game:

When I first looked at the rules, I was expecting a fairly dull game; lots of moves where nothing happens except the addition of pegs to pods, or the slow advance of pods across the board. I was wrong - the jumping, and potentially great manoeuvrability of the pods, means that when pods do move, they can sometimes cross the board in a single move, possibly capturing several pods along the way. The most common move is adding a prong to a pod, but even these moves are full of tension, because the addition of a single prong can cause a complete re-evaluation of a position.

[...] All in all, an excellent game with good production - vinyl board and wooden components - as you'd expect from the Great American Trading Company. The sheer number of options available makes things very hard for a poor computer, which is probably going to have to consider around 100 moves per turn in a reasonably complex position (compared to around 30 in chess), yet a human player can play intuitively and ignore the majority of these options. Still, computers are getting faster every day, so I don't see us humans keeping the edge for very long!

Another game that uses the prongs idea is 2005's Stlts.
