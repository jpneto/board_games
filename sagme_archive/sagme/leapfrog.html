Leapfrog

Date: 2030-03-08 13:36 | Tags: older games, checkers

Leapfrog is, according to H. J. R. Murray, a traditional English game:

A History of Board-games other than Chess (1952) pgs.92-3

So, the game can be played on any square board and the original setup is filled with pieces of the same color. The variant proposed by Murray in 1898 places a random set of pieces of four colors,

the suggested proportion of green/red/yellow/white pieces is 1:2:3:4

Rules

  • Initially, each player captures one piece from the board
  • Afterwards, in her turn, the player selects a piece and jumps over an orthogonally adjacent piece, landing on the immediate next square that must be empty
    • the player captures the jumped piece
    • jumps are multiple and mandatory (as in Checkers)
    • the player must not choose the option that maximizes captures
  • When there are no more valid moves, the player with the highest score wins
    • green pieces value four points, red three points, yellow two points, and white one point

This is a game of attention, not strategy. The interaction with the adversary is non-existent. The player needs to consider how to maximize the score of the current move minus the adversary's next move (more than 2-ply becomes hard to evaluate). The game is impartial, all game options are shared by both players, so there is not much to do except to hope the adversary makes sub-optimal choices.

One possible modification, that would create some asymmetry, is to state that the color of the last captured piece is the color the adversary must pick next. If the adversary cannot move, the game ends and wins the highest scorer, as in the original ruleset.

The game is quite similar to Konane. Given the many voyages of British ships over the Pacific in the 18th and 19th centuries, a connection could be possible, i.e., that Leapfrog could have been inspired by Konane's rules.
