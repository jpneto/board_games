Floating Islands

Date: 2027-04-09 08:00 | Tags: old games

Floating Islands, or Schwimmende Inseln, is a 1982 game by Martin Kastenholz, published at Avantgarde-Spiele.

The game is played on a 20x20 board,

The following rules were taken from the only source available (to my knowledge): the old game's site from the Wayback Machine at Feb 12, 2003.

Material:

  • There are 60 square pieces (the island stones). Islands are formations made up of adjacent island stones. The entire gameplay takes place on the islands. At the beginning of the game, all islands, except one (the island in the center), consist of four stones. 
  • The round playing pieces are the motors. Each player starts with one motor and can later have up to nine. These motors are used to move and steer the islands. Motors are always on islands, never in the water.
  • The wooden cones are the runners. Each player has two runners, which also remain only on islands and never in the water.

The eight spaces in the center with atom symbols are the power fields. Each power field is surrounded by three or four highlighted charging fields.  

Important: Two spaces are adjacent if they share a common side. Spaces that only touch at a corner are not adjacent. Two island stones are adjacent if they are located on adjacent spaces. Adjacency is always orthogonal.

Game Setup:

  • The game board has 60 spaces marked with black squares, forming 13 "islands." 
  • In a 4-player game, each of these spaces receives one island stone, creating eight numbered starting islands and five "neutral" islands.  
    • In a 3-player game, the island numbered 4 is not set up.  
    • In a 2-player game, the islands numbered 2 and 4 are not set up. 
    • Thus, the total number of stones used: 60 stones (4 players), 52 stones (3 players), 44 stones (2 players)
  • Each player selects two starting islands with the same number and sits on the corresponding side of the table. They take two runners and one motor of the same color and place them on the designated spaces of their starting islands:  
    - Left starting island → One runner and one motor  
    - Right starting island → One runner  
  • Finally, the remaining motors are placed on the power fields. Each power field receives one motor from each player, forming motor stacks of a height equal to the number of players. The order of these stacked motors does not matter. These motors are initially inactive and only come into play later. In the rules, "motors" always refer to active motors on islands. 

Objective:

  • Each player must move both of their runners to all power fields as quickly as possible. The first player to achieve this wins the game. 
  • Runners can only move on islands, and islands can only move with the help of motors. However, motors can not only be used to help one's own runners but also to obstruct opponents.  

Fundamental Laws:

  • Law 1: An island is a group of at least four adjacent island stones. A island can grow to any size.
  • Law 2: A single island stone can hold:
    • At most one motor and/or
    • At most one runner (on top of a motor). 
    • A stone can have both a motor and a runner, but they do not need to belong to the same player. 
  • Law 3: A player may have a maximum of three motors on a single island.  
    • However, multiple players can have motors on the same island, so an island may have more than three motors in total.  

Gameplay:

  • Each turn consists of two actions:  
  1. Move runners (in any order). 
  2. Use motors to transport island stones (in any order), but both runners must move before activating motors.  

Moving Runners:

Runners move step by step. A step means moving to an adjacent island stone. 

  • A runner may move as many steps as the number of their own motors on the island.  
  • A runner can move fewer steps than allowed or remain in place.  
  • If the island contains none of the player's motors, the runner cannot move.  
  • If both runners are on the same island, their combined steps cannot exceed the number of the player's motors on that island.
  • A runner can move over motors (including opponent's motors).  
  • A runner cannot end its movement on another runner's space.  


Exceptions: "Poor Runner Rule"  

  • A runner on an island with only one of its own motors (thus only able to move one step) is considered a poor runner.  
  • It may step onto a space occupied by an opponent's runner as long as it immediately moves away with an extra step.  
  • The extra step must be used immediately.  
  • A poor runner cannot use this rule to gain multiple extra steps in a row.  
  • This rule does not apply to runners of the same color.  

Transporting Island Stones 

After moving runners, a player may use each of their motors to transport one island stone: 

  1. Move the stone the motor is sitting on (its base).  
  2. Move another stone from the same island. 

How it works:  

  • In case a, the motor and its stone are moved together to an adjacent empty space.  
  • In case b, the motor is lifted off its stone and placed on a new stone before transporting it.  

Restrictions: A motor cannot move a stone if

  1. The stone is underneath another motor.  
  2. The stone is underneath a runner.  
  3. Moving the stone would violate Law 1 (leaving behind an island with fewer than four stones).  
  • Exception: A four-stone island may temporarily shrink to three stones while moving but must not split into two- or one-stone islands.  
  • If a motor cannot transport a stone, it must remain on its base and cannot be placed elsewhere.  

Merging/Splitting Islands:

  • Merging: If transporting a stone connects two islands, they form a single, larger island. Motors can now move stones from either island.  
  • Splitting: Moving a stone may split an island into multiple islands, but each new island must have at least four stones (Law 1).  
  • A nine-stone island is the smallest that can be split.  

Short Circuit:

  • If a merger results in a player having more than three motors on an island, the excess motors are immediately removed from the game.  
  • Players take turns clockwise removing their excess motors until all islands comply with Law 3. The player who caused the short circuit removes first.  

Activating a new Motor

  • If a runner moves onto or over a charging field and has an inactive motor in the corresponding power field, the motor must be activated immediately.  
  • The player takes the motor and places it on a motor-free stone of the island that reached the power field. The new motor is fully charged and can transport a stone immediately.  
    • If all stones on the island already have motors, the activated motor is lost.  
    • If the player already has three motors on the island, the motor is lost (Law 3).  
    • Power fields cannot be covered by island stones, and runners cannot enter them.  

Winning the Game

  • The first player to reach all power fields (i.e., has no more inactive motors left) wins.  
  • There are no ties—if two players reach their last power field in the same round, the first player wins.  

A review of this game:

The rules of Floating Islands sound interesting, and the unusual game components are noteworthy. Unfortunately, the actual gameplay is not convincing.  

A four-player game is acceptable since players interfere with each other, removing island tiles to separate opponents from their motors or trapping them. However, with fewer players, the game becomes dull. A two-player game, in particular, often results in players wandering back and forth without much interaction. Even the strategic advice in the rulebook does little to improve this.  

The game duration is quite long. The early stages drag because each player starts with only one motor, limiting movement to a single piece. It takes considerable time before both pieces are equipped with two motors.  

Conclusion: A potentially interesting idea that still needs refinement.
