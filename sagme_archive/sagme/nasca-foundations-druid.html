Nasca, Foundations, Druid

Date: 2028-09-01 09:35 | Tags: new games, tile games

Nasca is a 1998 game by Corné van Moorsel, published at Cwali.

The game is played on a 10x11 board:

Each player has twelve tiles: three 1x5, four 1x4, three 1x3, and two 1x2. 

There are also six neutral pieces, one 1x3, two 1x2, and three 1x1

Initially the neutral pieces can be placed on the board, to serve as foundations for the remaining pieces.

Rules:

  • On his turn, the player drops one friendly tile, with the following conditions:
    • must be placed on the board's squares or on top of other pieces 
    • must stay horizontally wrt the board surface
    • at least one of the supporting pieces must be of the same color, or both supports are neutral
    • cannot extend beyond the board's edges
    • touches only one square from each tile below it
    • can serve as a bridge over existing pieces but only its middle can touch another tile below
    • cannot be placed under a tile already on board
  • If a player does not have a valid move, he passes his turn
  • When tiles (and board edges) of the player enclose empty squares, one or more territories are created; each empty square values 1 point
    • a territory may contain a smaller opponent's territory. The player who controls the inner area scores the points for the empty squares in that area. The player who controls the outer area scores the points corresponding to the remaining empty squares, which are not part of the inner area.
  • When both players pass, wins the player with more territory

The rules suggest playing two matches and then sum the total of both, before declaring a winner.

Notice that territories can be unmade by the adversary placing higher level tiles.

Another similar game is 2004's Druid by Cameron Browne.

From BGG description:

On a square board players take turns placing cubes (sarsens) which may be played on the ground or on any piece of your color, and (3x1) rectangles (lintels) which can be placed in such a way that they are supported at both ends and are supported by exactly two blocks of your color and optionally one block of the opponent's color. The simplest configuration therefore resembles a mini Stone Henge (hence the name). The space in between can be empty or occupied by a piece of either color.

One player is attempting to connect the top and bottom of the board and the other player the left and right edges. Connectivity is judged as seen from above so that higher pieces can cut the connection of lower ones.

The author suggests that, for a 10x10 board, each player needs 100 cubes, 20 1x2 units, and 10 1x3 units. Check the author's page for complete rules of Druid.
