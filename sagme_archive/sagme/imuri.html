Imuri

Date: 2030-01-04 20:29 | Tags: old games, connection

Imuri is a 1959 game by Klaus Munro, published at Rhön-plastik (which wasn't a game publisher).

The game is played in a 30x30 board: 

This is a connection game, that uses pins and walls (as seen in the initial game cover), to establish paths. Each player places a friendly wall on their turn (together with the necessary pins). The goal is to connect two opposite side (north-south for one player, east-west for the other).

Many readers might recognize these rules from a much more famous game: 1962's Twixt:

Twixt is played on a smaller 24x24 board

From a forum discussion at littlegolem:

The game now mostly known under the name of TWIXT, was invented (at least) two times independently of each other, under different names. One was Twixt, by Alex Randolph. The other one was “imuri”, by Klaus Munro from Hamburg. 

[...] years ago I did some detective work to find out about the origins of TWIXT/imuri. The best answers I got from Johannes Ruettinger. He was working in the company which published imuri. Later he opened his own company (3 Magier Spiele) where Alex Randolph published some of his games. According to Ruettinger the game was played (in Bavaria) already around 1953 by schoolboys with paper and pencil. Then, Randolph and Munro must have invented “material versions” of it independently of each other: Randolph in Vienna (in 1958), Munro in a beach holiday in Yugoslavia (in 1959).  

I also had a long phone call with Munro. One day, around 1967, he came in a shop (in Hamburg?!) and found TWIXT (3m edition). He became very annoyed, because he thought that it was some clone of imuri. On the other hand he was very impressed because he saw that the realization (with the board, and the towers to be pinned in the holes) was much better than in imuri. Munro made a lot of money with his song texts (for Vicky Leandros, Roger Whittaker, many others). So, for him the game playing scene (and the question who had how much percents in the invention of the game) lost all importance. For Randolph it must have been important to document his beginnings with TWIXT. “Kosmos Verlag”, which published a TWIXT vesion only a few years ago, also printed a little booklet where the very first games of TWIXT - between A. Randolph and Herbert Feuerstein in 1958 - are documented.

However, at this BGG forum:

This claim by Munro was refuted by Alex Randolph over 32 years ago in Spielwelt. [...] Because there were other inquiries, we wanted to know and asked our Twixt-author Alex Randolph to make a short statement. Here it is:

"It is often surprising and strange that in the realm of science, technology, and games the same thing can be invented independently at the same time in two or more versions. It is an interesting subject about which I would like very much to write a longer article. In the case of Twixt/lmuri the matter is different. It is very hard for me to believe that the similarity between Twixt and Imuri is only a puzzling and quite coincidental meeting, mainly because Twixt is so much older. In 1958 Twixt was already known to gaming experts such as Martin Gardner and Sid Sackson. 

The beautiful 3M edition was on the market in America in 1962, many years before Imuri. It was a giant success, so much that I have spent almost ten years living very pleasantly off the royalties. In the 60s, it was also sold in England and France and most likely in German specialty stores. At that time there was no trace of Imuri. I myself, first saw Imuri in 1971 when I was still living in Japan. On a European vist I went to Ravensburg, and a copy of Imuri was shown to me by Erwin Glonnegger, who of course had known my Twixt for years. I immediately wrote a cordial letter to the publisher of Imuri that remained unanswered, and I followed with a report to 3M in America. That was all years ago. In 1972 Imuri appeared in Germany, but only a short time later it disappeared from the market."
