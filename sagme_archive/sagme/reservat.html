Reservat

Date: 2028-09-15 14:48 | Tags: new games, tile games

Reservat is a 1998 game by Ekkehard Künzell, and published at Logika.

The game comes with all 29 different pentacubes,

The box where they came is also part of the game. Its size is 8x8x2 with a volume of 128 units cubes. The total volume of all pentacubes is 29*5 = 145 unit cubes. The difference is 17 units of volume (it's no coincidence the seventeen cubes that didn't fit in the box at the previous image).

Rules:

  • Initially, each player on his turn, selects one of the available pentacubes, until each player has twelve pieces (the remaining five pieces are not used)
  • On the next phase, each player places one pentacube inside the box.
    • the piece cannot extend beyond the edge of the box (so, a maximum height of two)
    • the piece's surface must fit in the board's grid
    • the piece must be stable, i.e., it is supported by the board and/or other pieces, and must remain at an orthogonal angle
    • previous pieces are assumed immovable
    • a piece might slide at the board by placing it in the grid, and then make orthogonal moves to fit it in place
  • Wins the player that makes the last move
    • if players want scoring the matches, the winner scores an extra point for each piece he can insert after the game ends

The author, Ekkehard Künzell, wrote a booklet Spiele mit Pentakuben (1971), with several games that can be played with pentacubes,

Some tactical considerations from this work:

  • Each player will endeavor to create reserves. These are empty spaces not yet filled, but so delimited by other pieces that only one piece from their own collection fits in. A reserve and the fitting game piece are reserved for the endgame to be able to still place when the other parts of the game box are already occupied. It is the constant task of the players to see through construction plans for reserves of the opponent and to prevent their implementation if possible or to compensate by building their own reserves.
  • The choice of a favorable and "fitting together" collection is an important prerequisite for a successful game. After just a few games, the beginner will recognize that reserves can be built particularly easily for pieces 60, 90, and 61, 37. These four pieces are therefore often chosen first. Furthermore, an experienced player pays attention to ensuring that not all long pieces (10, 11, 12, 40) are in one hand, otherwise large edge strips can easily be delimited, into which no piece from one's own collection fits.
  • When placing pieces, two main playing styles can be distinguished: the closed and the open.
    • In the closed playing style, the players place their pieces as close as possible to the previously placed pentacubes to prevent space for reserves from arising. For a player who pushes for this style of play, it is also difficult to create reserves for himself. Since little dead space arises in this more defensive style of play, the point gains in such games are low.
    • In the open playing style, the pieces are deliberately placed with gaps. Instead of destroying, the motto is: If you create a reserve for yourself there, then I will construct one here for myself. Such a game usually ends with a high point gain for the winner. In the course of a game, the playing style changes depending on the situation.
  • Since a single piece alone cannot form a reserve, it is important to cleverly exploit the respective situation. It is particularly clever to succeed in creating two possibilities for building a reserve with one move. Such a move can only be parried by simultaneously preventing one possibility of the co-player while also preparing one's own reserve to compensate for the other possibility of the co-player.
  • Whoever has more reserves than the other is always at an advantage. With an equal number of reserves, the one who is forced first to utilize one of their reserves for placing is the loser. The endgame thus requires the blocking of all other placement options of the playing partner.
