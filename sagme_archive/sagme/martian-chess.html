Martian Chess

Date: 2028-08-04 12:41 | Tags: new games

Martian Chess is a 1995 game by Andrew Looney, published at Looney Labs.

The game is played on two 4x4 board sectors,

initial setup using the game's original target: the Icehouse set

Each player starts with three big pieces (the queens), three middle (the drones) and three small (the pawns). Given the rules of merging, it's best to use pieces that can easily change value,


using dice instead

Rules:

  • Piece movement:
    • the pawns move one step diagonally
    • the drones move one or two steps orthogonally
    • the queens move like the Chess Queen
  • On his turn, the player either:
    • moves one of his pieces 
      • each player only control the pieces on his 4x4 sector, so changing sector means changing ownership
      • a piece can move to an empty square, or to a square occupied by an opponent's piece, which is then captured
      • if the adversary moved a piece to the player's sector in the previous turn, it is illegal to undo that same move (i.e., moving it back to the same square)
    • moves a drone into a friendly pawn (or vice versa), merging them into a queen, provided that the player does not have any queens
    • moves a pawn into a friendly pawn, merging them into a drone, provided that the player does not have any drones
  • The game ends when a player does not have more pieces on his sector
  • Wins the player with the highest score
    • each captured pawn values 1 point, each drone 2 points, each queen 3 points
    • if players have the same score, wins the player that moved last
A review from Abstract Games Magazine #13:
 
Play of this game can be very confusing initially. One is apt to forget that a piece moved over to the other side to effect a capture may simply move straight back to recapture. It is probably bad positioning, therefore, to have two large pyramids on the same file, since whatever you can capture with the foremost, the opponent can capture the other large pyramid right back. Also, there may be a tendency among Chess players to feel reluctant about moving the larger pieces into opposing territory. Nevertheless, it is captured pieces that count, not pieces on the board, and a large pyramid on the other side may prove to be a useful target. In fact, if you are ahead, shifting pieces into the opposing camp as quickly as possible is an effective endgame strategy since the game ends as soon as you cannot move. Martian Chess is a mind-bending game [...]
 
Ralg Gering explains at AG #14 that the Ko rule is not strong enough to solve endgame problems where a player is able to avoid the end of the game, if that means losing. He suggests (correctly, imho) that a better rule at dealing with these positions, would be to give all remaining pieces on board to the player that finishes the game.
 
Another option would be to add a rule (similar to FIDE Chess) stating that, after N moves without captures, the game ends.

Despite the game's name, this is not a Chess variant.
