Kontact

Date: 2028-04-28 07:50 | Tags: old games, stack pieces

Kontact is a undated game (20th century) by Jean-Louis Marti, published at Dujardin.


The game is played on a 9x9 board,

Each player has

  • ten rectangular "ramparts"
  • ten square "dungeons"
  • ten round "towers"
  • ten pyramidal "roofs"

Rules:

  • In the first phase (the Foundation), each player drops five ramparts in turn
    • The first rampart must be placed on two light-colored squares
    • The next ramparts must be placed adjacent to a previous one (of either color), either on its small or large side
    • When all ten ramparts are placed on the board, each player must place, on his turn, five additional pieces (either ramparts, dungeons, or towers)
      • Each placed piece must always touch another piece (on the side, not diagonally)
  • For the next phase (the Construction), on his turn, the player either:
    • drops a new friendly piece (from the reserve) on empty squares adjacent to opponent's pieces
    • takes one of their pieces, already on the board, and places it over an opponent’s piece
      • A rampart can be placed over an adjacent enemy rampart or over two adjacent pieces (tower or dungeon) being at least one from the adversary
      • A dungeon can be placed horizontally over an adjacent enemy tower or rampart
      • A tower can be placed over an enemy rampart, tower, or dungeon, using a diagonal move
    • places one friendly roof directly on top of a construction with height of three or more, if it contains at least one opponent’s piece [given the stacking rules, it seems to me that this always happens]
  • Wins the player that places all ten roofs


rampart, dungeons and tower movements

Some notes:

  • A piece can never be placed directly on another piece of the same type.
  • A piece must first be placed on an empty square before being stacked.
  • However, a piece can move up a construction if other pieces have already been placed underneath it (as long as the squares are connected).
  • A structure must always be made up of pieces of different colors.
  • However, it is not necessary to alternate colors when placing a roof.
  • Players may place a piece of the same color on top of another piece to set up an annexation strategy—allowing them to cover an opponent’s piece on the next move before their opponent can react.

The rules are quite extensive and don't feel natural (even the medieval theme seems strange while stacking pieces). Here's a match explaining part of the game dynamics, from the original rules (taken from L’Escale à Jeux rules):

[the translation] To help you understand the game, repeat the opening moves played by Jacqueline and Jean-Louis, and then continue the game.

  • Jacqueline (J): Light-colored pieces
  • Jean-Louis (JL): Dark-colored pieces
The Foundations:
  • J and JL each play five ramparts, numbered 1 to 10.
  • J goes first, as she plays with the light-colored pieces.
  • J places the eleventh piece: a tower (11) at the corner of rampart (4).
  • JL responds by placing a dungeon (12) against tower (11).
    • If later, J places her tower (11) on rampart (4), then JL can place a tower on her dungeon (12).
  • Play continues in this way until the twentieth piece is placed.
  • The foundations are now complete.
Construction and Roof Placement:
  • J attacks: She places her tower (11) on rampart (4).
  • JL counterattacks: He places his dungeon (12) on tower (11).
  • J attacks again: She places dungeon (15) on rampart (6).
  • JL places a roof on his dungeon (12).
  • J attacks once more: She places dungeon (13) on tower (16).
  • JL places his dungeon (14) on rampart (7).
  • J makes a strong move: She moves dungeon (15) off rampart (6) to cover dungeon (14) of JL.
  • JL places rampart (8) on rampart (9).
  • J places a roof on her dungeon (15).
  • JL places tower (21) diagonally from dungeon (13) of J, which is on tower (16) of JL.

Now it’s up to you to finish the game for Jacqueline and Jean-Louis! We are sure you now understand the rules of Kontact, but we are still available to help resolve any uncertainties. © Jean-Louis Marti
