Alcazar and other Wall Games

Date: 2027-07-09 09:11 | Tags: old games, wall pieces, new games

Alcazar is a 1978 game by Martin Davison, published at Otto Maier Verlag and Ravensburger.

The board is a 4x4 grid, where walls are placed between squares. 

This is the initial setup:

Each player has one piece (the knight). All the walls are shared. The knight's castle consists of the three initial squares inside the walls.

Rules:

  • On her turn, the player either:
    • Moves her knight to an orthogonally adjacent empty square which does not have a wall separating them
      • Players must move their knights at least once until the third turn  
      • A knight cannot move back into its starting square
    • Rotates a wall over one of its towers
      • the rotation is only valid if the wall, while rotating, does not cross over other walls
      • the rotation is also invalid if it goes through a square holding a knight
      • rotations can be 90º, 180º or 270º for either direction (which includes rotating outside the board)
  • Wins the player that either:
    • reaches the opponent's starting square, 
    • blocks the opponent's knight outside its own castle (i.e., a blocked knight is one that cannot move due to walls, the board edge and/or the opponent's knight)

An earlier related game is 1975's Cul-de-Sac, aka Blockade, by Philip Slater and published at Lazy Days.

From the BGG description:

Players each have two pawns, nine green walls (which are placed vertically), and nine blue walls (placed horizontally). Pawns start on the color matching them, which are 4x4 squares in on each of the four corners of the 11x14 board. The object is simple: to get one of your pawns to a starting spot of your opponent. Each turn players can move one pawn up to two spaces, and also place one wall, useful for blocking off their opponent's move. Walls always cover two squares worth of area, and must be placed according to their color (vertically or horizontally). Once players are out of walls, they keep moving pawns until one wins.

Of course, pawns cannot go over walls. In Cul-de-Sac the two pawns can also share the same square, while in Blockade (renamed for a new edition) pawns can jump over the other. There seem to be rule variations on some details.

Alcazar and Cul-de-Sac are precursors of 1997's Quoridor by Mirko Marchesi. Quoridor is a better known game -- and a better game than Alcazar, with less ad-hoc rules and a more abstract theme (i.e., no theme).



The success of Quoridor, where its precursors failed, is another example that each game seems to have its own proper timing, an appropriate artistic expression, and good marketing to succeed in a business where it is so easy to fail.
 
§
 
Quoridor is a re-implementation of 1995's Pinko Pallino by the same designer, which was later licensed to Gigamic.
 

 
The main difference was the ability to move diagonally, which makes wall placement less relevant. The later change to orthogonal movements was a good design decision.

Another, older, related game is Τα τείχη, aka, The Walls, published at Dourios in 1980.
 

Played on a 10x10 board, the goal is to create the biggest wall path. This is different from the usual blocking theme of the other wall games.
 
Here are the official rules:
 
 
The automatic translation from Greek:

THE WALLS

Play is for 2–4 players. A rectangular playing field of dimensions 10 x 10 to 15 x 15 is required, depending on the number of players. Each player has a set of walls of a specific color.

Each wall is 1 x 2 units in size and is placed on the board to block movement paths. The game begins with the field empty. In turn, each player places one wall on the board, aiming to create a continuous barrier (a wall) from one side of the board to the opposite side (top to bottom or left to right, depending on the player's direction). Walls may not overlap or extend beyond the boundaries of the board. Players may attempt to block the progress of their opponents by strategically placing walls.

The challenge is that there is no single correct route. Strategy and prediction are key. Players must anticipate opponents’ moves while building their own path. If a player manages to create a continuous wall from their starting side to the opposite side, they win the game.

In a 3-player game, the third player acts as a “saboteur,” trying to prevent any one player from completing their wall, and wins if neither of the other two players succeeds. In a 4-player game, two opposing teams can be formed (each team sitting opposite), and the goal becomes cooperative, to help one's teammate complete the wall.

The game develops spatial reasoning, logical thinking, and tactics. It’s ideal for short games and travel, due to its simplicity and portability. It’s suitable for all ages and for use in educational environments.
