Chikara

Date: 2026-12-04 10:48 | Tags: old games

Chikara is a 1989 game by Mark Craig and Nick Fletcher, published at Manik Games.

The game is played on a grid of size 10x13,


      the player's first line is called the defense line

Each player has 26 pieces/sticks of one color.

Definitions 

  • a stronghold is a sequence of sticks that enclose an area of the board. The smallest area is a 1x1 square, requiring four sticks.
  • a graph is a set of sticks of the same color that are connected to each other via the grid, that are not part of a stronghold.

Rules [rules reconstructed from the available information]

  • Initially, each player places ten friendly sticks on their defensive lines, either horizontally or vertically.
  • On his turn, the player places a stick that must be connected to another friendly stick already on board.
    • If all sticks are already on board, then players must reposition one of their sticks, that again must be connected to another friendly stick
    • All enemy graphs that touch the current moved stick are captured; in the event of a capture, the moved stick is also removed from play
  • If a player creates a stronghold, the friendly sticks inside it can also the repositioned in this same turn (or after)
    • note: relocating sticks inside a stronghold might create a new stronghold, and the relocation rules apply again
  • The player that places a friendly stick over the adversary's defense line (so, the stick must be placed horizontally), wins the game

An excerpt from Richard Breese's review:

[...] These strongholds provide many interesting tactical alternatives. There is also a good balance between the advantage of building up significant fire power by way of a large stronghold containing, say, ten reserve sticks, and the subsequent disadvantage of having to break down the empty stronghold. The disadvantage arises because any stick which does not form part of a stronghold and which is touched by an opponent's stick is immediately and permanently removed from the board. A large, partly dismembered stronghold, can therefore be taken in its entirety if an opponent is able to reach it with one of their own sticks.

Unless a player has sacrificed material for a positional advantage, an experienced player will usually be able to convert a one stick advantage into a victory. As a result, I have seen various movement sequences developed which limit the number of vulnerable sticks (those not forming part of a stronghold) at any given time to one. Because the sticks once taken are not returned to the board the playing time is limited naturally. A game tends to last between ten to fifteen minutes and I have not yet known a game to be drawn. In the game an initial jostle for position is often followed by a single strike, using several reserve sticks. If the strike fails then the attacker's defence is often too weak to survive. An alternative to a single strike strategy is to advance one or two mini strongholds in order to try and stretch your opponent's defence, but without losing any sticks.

Assuming the complete rules are not that different from what this post proposes, this seems like a very promising game. The branching factor can become enormous, despite the game's clarity. I would not be surprised that these rules are able to produce a great deal of depth.
