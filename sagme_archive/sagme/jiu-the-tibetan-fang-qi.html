Jiu, The Tibetan Fang Qi

Date: 2030-10-18 21:05 | Tags: traditional games

On a previous post about Diu Fang, I mentioned several possible rulesets based on partial translations and evidence from secondary sources.

I asked a BGG user, Mingyang Tian, to help me better understand this game. He answered:

Actually Diu Fang (or Fang Qi/方棋, which is the most common name) is not a game, but a family of games (like mancala), and the specific rules varies with different places.

The common feature is that when you form a square by moving your stones, you can capture an opponent's stone. Only the smallest square counts, that is, four stones adjacent to each other on the four corners of a cell. (In some variants you can also capture by leaping, like in draughts)

This is an important piece of the puzzle to understand what seemed a game with too many different possible rules. The evidence is referring to several different games from the same family!

Tian included a mention to the Tibetan variant, called Jiu.

14*14 board

Setup: put one blacks stone at G7, one white stone at H8

Phase1: Drop one stone each turn, until the board is full. Then remove the two initial stones.

Phase2: Each turn: 

1) Move a stone one step orthogonally, or
2) Jump over one enemy-stone orthogonally to an empty space, and capture that enemy-stone. Can repeat as many times as possible.

After your move, if four of your stones form a 2*2 square, then you can capture an enemy-stone. You can choose anyone.

Here are two basic patterns called "褡裢", that allows you to capture a piece on every turn, until it's destroyed.

Endgame: Capture a certain number of enemy-stones.

阿坝地区流行的藏棋”久“

在四川阿坝流行的藏棋称为“久”，与“密芒”为贵族阶层所好不同的是，“久”更民间化一些。“久”的棋子根据对弈者置身环境，就地取材，或石子、或土块、或小木棍皆可用，没有固定或统一的形状和颜色，只要区分开双方就行。现在则多采用围棋棋子，美观大方。这或许就是“久”能在藏区得以广泛流传的主要原因。“久”的棋盘无定制，往往随地画就若干纵线和横线，规格因人而异，有7线格、8线格、10线格、14线格不等。

目前，阿坝正式的藏棋为14线格。开局之前，在棋盘中心方格的对角上放一个白子和黑子，然后由白棋先下开始布局，在布局阶段双方轮流落子，互相缠绕，尽量阻止对方走成四方形，棋盘完全填满后布局才告一阶段。接着首先去掉最先下在四方形对角的两个棋子，有了两个空隙，就进入战斗的阶段了，此时由黑子先下，像下跳棋一样，隔着对方的棋子跳过去并吃掉对方隔挡之子，也可以顺着交叉点，一步一步地走，一旦自己的棋子走成了四方形，将赢得任意去掉对方一个棋子的机会，最后谁剩的棋子多谁赢。

[translation] 'Jiu' A Tibetan Chess Variant Popular in the Aba Region

In the Aba region of Sichuan, a variant of Tibetan chess known as “Jiu” is popular. Unlike “Mimang,” which was favored by the aristocracy, “Jiu” is more grassroots and folk-oriented. The pieces used in “Jiu” are often made from readily available materials, depending on the environment of the players — stones, clumps of earth, or small wooden sticks can all serve as pieces. There is no fixed or standardized shape or color for the pieces; as long as the two sides can be distinguished, that’s sufficient. Nowadays, Go pieces are commonly used for their clean and elegant appearance. This adaptability may be one of the main reasons “Jiu” has spread widely throughout the Tibetan regions.

The board for “Jiu” is not standardized either. It is often drawn directly on the ground with intersecting vertical and horizontal lines. The number of lines can vary — 7, 8, 10, or even 14 lines, depending on the players.

Currently, the official version of Tibetan chess in Aba uses a 14-line grid. At the beginning of the game, one white piece and one black piece are placed diagonally in the center square of the board. Then, white moves first to begin the setup phase. In this phase, players alternate placing pieces, trying to entangle each other and prevent the opponent from forming a square shape. This setup phase ends when the board is completely filled.

Next, the two initial pieces placed diagonally in the center are removed, creating two empty spaces. This marks the beginning of the battle phase. In this phase, black moves first. Players can jump over their opponent’s pieces like in checkers, capturing them, or they can move step by step along the intersections. If a player forms a square with their own pieces, they earn the right to remove one of the opponent’s pieces. In the end, the player with the most remaining pieces wins.
